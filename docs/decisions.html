AI_news: recent decisions

What changed since v0.6, and why. The architecture diagram and its block cards already reflect these.

DecisionSinceWhy
Your fit is measured against every open role, not only the best fitsv1.11The openings are the best fits by design, so the share of their skills you cover is well above the market's, and read alone it suggests your gaps are small. The search itself never used your skills (titles, markets and fixed AI search terms only), and the model reads each posting without your profile, so the bias was only in what was shown. The openings now say they are “the best 8 of N” and compare their coverage with a typical role's. The weekly report's “Your fit” now starts with how much of a typical open role's skills you cover, across every open role in the target families, each role once, whatever its date or level: overall, for technical skills only and per role family, so a family where the gap is large shows. The demand-weighted coverage of the top 30 skills comes after it and says it runs higher: the most-asked skills are the common ones, and most gaps sit in the long tail it leaves out.
The voice overview is 8 minutes long, read at a teacher's even pacev1.10Once each story was explained, the brisk podcast pace (about 16.5 characters a second) felt too fast to follow. The voice now reads like a teacher explaining new ideas, at a moderate, even pace with a short pause between paragraphs: about 13 characters a second, and steady from one call to the next (12.1 to 13.8 in eight readings, where a “moderate” podcast host read anywhere from 10 to 14), as long as each request is short: the overview is read in parts of about 1,800 characters, in parallel, because parts of 3,100 slowed to 10.8 and made an 8-minute script last 11. The same script takes longer at that pace, so the overview is now 8 minutes (AINEWS_VOICE_MINUTES) and the narrator's word target follows the measured pace, about 112 words a minute, so it says about as much as before, only slower. The narrator wrote anywhere from 900 to 1,150 words for that target, so the code now holds it to the length: while the script is more than 5% over, it leaves out a part's second sentence, never its first, starting with the details of what happened in the top stories, then the second sentence of the simple explanation, minor stories first. About $0.13 a brief. The narrator's number check now also reads numbers the brief spells out (“six point eight billion” for 6.8 billion): the editor sometimes writes them in words, the narrator writes digits, and the check had dropped those sentences as unsupported, which cut a story's first sentence.
The voice overview explains each story in simple words and says why it matters for youv1.9Many of the stories are new to the reader, who listens first and reads only what interests them, so the overview now explains as well as reports. Each story has three parts, written separately and joined by the code: what happened, the idea in simple words (from the brief's “What is it?”, its plain intro and analogy only, because “How it works” is too deep to follow by ear), and why it matters for the reader's own experience and work (from “Comment from a pro” and the profile). Spoken signposts such as “In simple words” or “Since you've built…” vary from story to story. Gemini 3.8 Flash writes it (about $0.015) because Flash-Lite repeated one lead-in twelve times and tied stories to the reader less; both wrote two sentences where one was asked, so the code keeps at most one or two per part, which holds the overview to about 7 minutes. The code also keeps the brief's order and reads the title of any story the model skips. The voice's style asks for a brisk, lively pace: a “calm” host read anywhere from 10 to 15 characters a second from one call to the next, so the same script took 7 or 10 minutes.
The voice message is a 6-minute spoken overview, not the whole brief read aloudv1.8Reading the whole brief aloud took 23 minutes, longer than reading it. The voice is for listening first, then reading only the stories that matter, so it now says what happened in about 6 minutes (AINEWS_VOICE_MINUTES). A narrator call on the editor's model writes the script from the verified brief, not the sources, so it can't bring in anything new; it costs under a cent. Every story is named by its number, in the brief's order, so it can be found in the text, and the overview ends with the two or three most worth reading. The prompt gives the length per story, because a total alone came out a third short, and says to keep common acronyms short, because the brief spells them out and “artificial intelligence agents” sounds wrong aloud. A sentence with a number the brief doesn't contain is dropped, as in the editor's check. If the narrator fails, the voice reads the headlines. The voice now costs about $0.10 a brief, so its caps go down to $0.30 a brief and $3 a month. What the voice said is kept with the brief (brief_speech).
The brief also goes out read aloud, as one Telegram voice message (Gemini 2.5 Flash TTS)v1.7Listening is easier than reading 15 stories on a phone. One voice message, not one per story: it plays straight through, and it goes first, so it is what you see when you open the channel, while the text messages keep the links. Gemini 2.5 Flash TTS on Vertex, like the other models: no new account or key, and about $0.40 for a 25-minute brief ($10 per 1M audio tokens, 25 a second), against nothing for a brief read on the phone. It reads a script made from the brief (links, emoji and coverage flags left out) in parts of about 4.5 minutes, four at a time, because one request produces at most about 11 minutes; the parts are joined with a short pause and encoded as OGG/Opus, the format Telegram plays as a voice message. The voice has caps of its own ($0.75 a brief, $6 a month, checked against the worst case before it starts), and the text caps leave it out, so it can never take the brief's or the jobs lane's budget; if it fails or would cross a cap, the brief goes out as text only. A redo now remembers the posts of the brief it replaces, so tg-clean removes them too.
Each opening shows its skills split by what you have and what would be new, with the size of the gap; the list ends with “Where you stand”; jobs and the brief every three daysv1.6A count of required skills said how close a posting was, but not which skills you bring or how far you are from the market. Each opening now names the skills you have, the required and nice-to-have ones that would be new to you, and the ones you're learning, with how much of its skills you cover, measured the same way as the fit, so the two always agree. “Where you stand” applies the same measure to all of the week's roles for you, each counted once, sent or not: your typical coverage, how many roles have a small, medium or big gap, the skills they ask for most that you have and that you lack, and how many roles the three most useful skills to learn would bring to a small gap. Code only, no model call, and each list keeps its numbers so the gap can be followed over time. Every three days runs on the 3rd, 6th, … 30th rather than cron's */3, which would run on the 31st and again on the 1st; the brief looks back up to five days to cover the longer gap after a month's last run.
Jobs and the brief every other day; the spending caps count your day, and the jobs lane leaves room for the briefv1.5On 14 Oct the brief went out without its notes: the caps counted a UTC day, which west of UTC starts the afternoon or evening before, so the previous evening's runs and the 05:00 jobs run had used $0.74 of the $0.75 before the 06:00 brief. A day now starts at local midnight, and the jobs lane, which runs first, stops reading postings while $0.15 of the day's cap is left (postings it skips wait for the next run). Every other day halves the cost and the posts; cron counts days of the month, so it runs on odd-numbered dates, and the brief looks back 50 hours instead of 26 so it still covers everything since the last one.
LangChain, LangSmith and podcast sources added; every link a feed lists is remembered, and a new source starts with a baselinev1.4YouTube's channel feeds return 404 for every channel, so podcasts and blogs stand in for talks: Dwarkesh, No Priors and Hamel Husain. The LangChain blog dates a post by its last edit, and the brief used to forget entries older than a week, so each edit would have brought an old post back as news. Remembering every listed link fixes that for all feeds, and a new feed's first fetch only records what is there. No Priors' feed links episodes only to the audio file, so it comes from Apple's public podcast directory, which has a page per episode. The GitHub releases of the LangChain monorepo and Deep Agents are left out: they ship patch releases almost daily, and the docs changelog covers the ones that matter.
Deployed as three Cloud Run jobs on Cloud Scheduler, the SQLite file in a bucket, set up with a gcloud scriptv1.3Jobs, not a service: nothing needs to answer requests, so nothing runs between the runs. Each run downloads the database, works on it and uploads a consistent copy only if no other run saved one in between, so two runs can never overwrite each other; the bucket keeps earlier copies as backups. One task and no retries, so a failed run never posts twice. A script first, because it is easy to read and run by hand; Terraform and GitHub Actions come later. Morning brief only for now, which keeps Scheduler within its three free triggers.
Daily openings shared between three employer groups: established, startups, top tierv1.2Ranked by fit alone, frontier labs and the best-known AI companies took most of the list: their postings match these skills best, and they are the hardest offers to get. Each employer now has a tier, set by hand in companies.yaml, and each group gets its own places (3, 3 and 2 by default). Places a group can't fill go to the others, but top tier never gets more than its share, so it stays a stretch goal rather than the whole list.
Daily openings ranked in code, sent once eachv1.1Fit comes from the fields the model already extracted: 70 points for skills, 30 for seniority. That costs nothing, gives the same answer every run, and lets each opening show which skills it rests on. The same role in several cities is shown once and no employer takes more than two places, so one big employer can't fill the list. The statistics block moves to the weekly lane (W1), where the code actually computes it.
Workday career sites added: 32 banks, insurers, pharma and big-tech employers, searched for AI rolesv1.0These employers don't use the simpler job-board APIs, and they hire many GenAI engineers and FDEs (Salesforce alone has FDE roles in five markets). robots.txt decides which career sites are used, so RBC's disallowed main site is left out. Hosts are searched four at a time to keep the daily run short.
Jobs lane starts with Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby: 97 employersv0.9Their public APIs return each employer's full list, so new and closed postings are exact. Workday search is ranked by relevance, so v1.0 searches within each tenant's own country filter and closes a posting only when its own page says it is gone.
Target roles and markets filtered by title and location, then refined by the modelv0.9Keeps about 1,600 of 13,000 postings. Solutions and research titles count only at AI companies or when the title names AI; the model then judges the role family from the duties.
Numbers computed in code, notes written by the modelv0.9Skills map to canonical names ("k8s" is Kubernetes), and every number in the report comes from the data. The model only explains, and its sentences are number-checked.
Rising and falling skills wait for two weeks of trackingv0.9During the first backfill the earlier period holds only postings still open; comparing it with this week would invent trends.
Gemini on Vertex AI instead of Claudev0.6Claude on Vertex needs an Anthropic access request with business details; Google's own models are available straight away. Claude stays as an optional provider.
Judge on Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, editor on 3.5 Flash-Litev0.7The cheapest model is enough for bulk scoring. Writing explanations benefits from a slightly stronger model, for 2–3 cents a brief. About $2–3 a month in total.
Spending guard in the app: an off switch plus per-run, per-day and per-month capsv0.6No surprise bills while tuning. Each call is refused before it is sent if its worst case would break a cap; failed calls are still logged with their cost.
Budget alerts in CAD on the shared project, no kill switchv0.6The billing account is in CAD, and unlinking billing would stop everything else in the project. Gemini has no per-project quota to lower, so the app's caps are the real-time limit.
A teaching-style brief, at most 15 itemsv0.8You're learning these topics. Each story has What happened, What is it? and a Comment from a pro written as your supervisor guiding your career.
A Trends & future of work section, with seven new sourcesv0.7Where AI is heading and how it changes tech work and jobs are the topics you most want. Trend stories are ranked without actionability, because there is rarely anything to do about a trend today.
One story per source; every source gets a candidate slotv0.7Stops one vendor's blog from filling the brief, and lets small sources reach the judge. The multi-outlet jobs search may lead three stories.
Article text for stories that arrive as headlinesv0.8A headline can't be explained. Pages are fetched once, robots.txt is honoured, and only the main text is kept. Paywalled pages and Google News links stay headline-only.
Whole stories packed into as few Telegram messages as fit, a contents list firstv0.8One message per story meant 15 notifications on the phone (silent ones still show). Packing up to Telegram's 4,096 characters fits 14 stories in 8 messages, and only the first one makes a sound.
Each story shows its date and how much of it the editor readv0.8You see how fresh a story is, and whether its summary comes from the full article, a paper abstract, an excerpt or only a headline, so you know when to open the link.
Every number checked in every paragraph and bullet, ignoring formattingv0.7The model can't add a number the sources don't contain, and "$0.30" vs "$0.3" no longer drops a correct sentence.
Keyword filters on broad search feedsv0.7The Reuters search matched any article mentioning AI: 40 of 113 items were off-topic. The jobs search now keeps only headlines about work and jobs.
Reddit: 30 s between requests and one retry after a minutev0.8Quick retries after a 429 only extended Reddit's block.
Test posts can be cleared from the channel (ainews tg-clean)v0.8Tuning runs left a channel full of old tries. The command keeps the latest sent brief, asks before deleting, and lists what Telegram wouldn't let it remove: a bot may delete only its own posts, and only within 48 hours.
Run by hand before deploying (ainews run, send, --redo)v0.8A few days of tuning quality first. --redo rebuilds the latest brief cheaply because judge scores are reused.
